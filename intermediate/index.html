Kapampangan Intermediate

B1–B2 · 22 lessons

The complete predication system, the sound rules, the particles, and the text

Where this picks up

The beginner course took you through A1 and A2: greetings, the markers, both pronoun sets, the fused pronouns, verb aspect, and actor and object focus.

This tier works through everything that was deferred — the rest of the predication types, the morphophonemics that let you read a form you have never met, the lexical particles, clause linking, the writing systems and the literature.

Why 22 lessons and not 24? B1 runs to twelve because Kapampangan's predication types and its sound changes genuinely need the room. B2 runs to ten because Kapampangan has far less contemporary media and register material than a language like Tagalog, and padding it to twelve would mean inventing. Ten honest lessons beat twelve with two of them hollow.

What this tier is built on

The primary source is Michael L. Forman, Kapampangan Grammar Notes (University of Hawai'i Press, PALI Language Texts, 1971) — a hundred-page reference grammar written from fieldwork in Bacolor between October 1968 and April 1969, and available free under a Creative Commons licence.

Every lesson maps to a section of it, and the examples are his. Where he hedges, these lessons hedge. Where he says something is not yet understood — and he does, more than once — the lesson says so too rather than inventing a tidy rule.

This tier does not analogise from Tagalog. That rule earned its keep: lesson 8 was drafted on the assumption that maka- is the potentive, as it is in Tagalog. Reading Forman showed it is a stative descriptive instead, and the lesson was rewritten. Shared vocabulary between the two languages makes the divergences more dangerous, not less.

A consequence worth stating plainly: what you learn here is the Kapampangan of one town, recorded over six months in 1969. Lesson 21 sets out what varies elsewhere.

A word on the audio. No browser ships a Kapampangan text-to-speech voice, so the 🔊 buttons use a Filipino (Tagalog) voice as a stand-in. Treat it as a rough guide, never a model — and note that it is least reliable on exactly the sound changes lessons 4 and 5 teach.
