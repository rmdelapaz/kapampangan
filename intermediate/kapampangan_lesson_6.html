Stative Predications with mi- and ka-

Being in a state rather than doing an action — and where kapatad comes from

  2. mi- statives

1. States, not actions

So far every predication you have met describes an action and picks which participant is in focus. Stative predications do something different: they say the ing-marked referent is in a certain state.

marked by mi- or ka- (also the circumfixes mi- … -an and ka- … -an)
+ an ing-marked noun phrase that is in that state

Most roots take one or the other. A few take both, and then the meanings diverge — with ka- usually carrying a causative sense.

2. mi- statives

Mirinan ding malan makasable. — The clothes hanging up got dirt on them.

Miabuan ye ing babi. — The pig got ashes on it.

Ika ing misasakitan. — You are the one who will suffer.

Mipatanan ya king pun ning mangga. — He was struck by the mango tree.

Mialbugan kami king Maliualu. — We were in Maliualu when sunset came.

Miaralan ka king amanu ning Dios. — You are schooled in the word of God.

Istung milamnan ka, sinulapo ka. — When you are filled up, you fly away.

Notice how many of these come out in English as passives — "got dirt on them," "was struck," "are schooled." That is an artefact of English. In Kapampangan they are not passives; they are statements that something is in a condition.

3. ka- statives and the causative sense

Where a root takes both prefixes, the contrast is sharp. Forman's example is takut, "fear":

FormSenseExample
katakutancausative — frightens someone Obat katakutan ye ing timbabalak?
Why does the lizard frighten you?
mitakutanstative — becomes frightened E ka mainge, pota mitakutan ya.
Don't make noise, or he'll get frightened.

More ka- statives:

Kebengian ya keng dalan. — He was overtaken by night on the road.

Kedagulan ne ing imalan na. — He got too big for his clothes.

Katubasan deng kamatis nung e me agad agtalan.
The tomatoes will get overripe if you don't pick them right away.

Kayabyayan daka. — We give and get from you.

Kebengian is worth a second look. It is ka- + bengi ("night") + -an, with the a → e change from lesson 4. "To be nighted" — English has no such verb, though it once did.

4. Reciprocals that count

Here is something Kapampangan does that will surprise you: for some roots, the mi- forms inflect for number.

FormHow manyExample
kapatethe one you do it withAtin kung metung a kapate.
There is one person I fight with.
mipatetwoAdua lang mipate.
The two of them fight each other.
mipapatethree or moreAtlu lang mipapate.
The three of them fight one another.

Reduplication marks the plural: mipate against mipapate. The same device you have seen marking aspect is here marking number.

Bisa kang mibarilan kata? — Do you want us to shoot each other?

5. Relationship words

mi- and ka- combine to express relationships — and this is where a word you learned in your first week comes from.

💡 kapatad is a ka- form

Beginner lesson 7 gave you kapatad, "sibling," as a plain vocabulary item. It is not plain. It is the same ka- relationship construction as kapate, "the one I fight with."

Forman lists it alongside tata, inda, bapa and dara as terms built on relationships. A sibling, structurally, is "the one you are co-something with."

The same ka- turns up in kaluguran (from lugud, "love") and kapitbale (from bale, "house"). Beginner vocabulary, intermediate morphology.

Forman gives a long list of roots that behave this way, including kumbira, ambun, bulad, dalig, gulis, sulat, uran, sipun, lumut and kutang.

6. Practice

Exercise 1: mi- or ka-?

Write mitakutan or katakutan.

  • "the lizard frightens you" uses ___ (Answer: katakutan)
  • "he'll get frightened" uses ___ (Answer: mitakutan)
  • The causative sense belongs to ___ (Answer: ka-|ka)

Exercise 2: How many?

  • Two people fighting each other: ___ (Answer: mipate)
  • Three or more: ___ (Answer: mipapate)
  • The one person you fight with: ___ (Answer: kapate)

Exercise 3: Break the word down

  • kebengian is built on the root ___ (Answer: bengi)
  • the a-to-e change there gives ka- as ___ (Answer: ke-|ke)
  • kapatad is built with the prefix ___ (Answer: ka-|ka)

Exercise 4: Complete Forman's sentences

  • "The pig got ashes on it." → "___ ye ing babi." (Answer: miabuan)
  • "You are the one who will suffer." → "Ika ing ___." (Answer: misasakitan)
  • "He got too big for his clothes." → "Kedagulan ne ing ___ na." (Answer: imalan)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is a stative the same as a passive? No. Passives rearrange who is in focus while keeping an action. Statives say something is in a condition. English often translates them alike, which is misleading.

How do I know whether a root takes mi- or ka-? Lexically, as usual. Where a root takes both, expect ka- to be causative.

Do all mi- forms inflect for number? No — Forman says "some roots." The reciprocal ones are where you will meet it.

Why This Lesson Matters

Statives cover a large slice of everyday description — being tired, dirty, caught out, rained on, frightened. And the ka- relationship pattern retroactively explains a handful of the most basic words in the language.
