Writing Systems

A syllabary, a Spanish alphabet and a Tagalog-inspired revision — and why people have strong opinions

1. Three systems

Beginner lesson 1 mentioned that older spellings exist. Forman lays out the full picture: three orthographic systems.

  1. the syllabary, ultimately derived from India
  2. the alphabetic writing introduced by the Spanish
  3. the revision of the alphabetic system inspired by Tagalog — the one this course uses

2. The syllabary

Early Spanish explorers reported widespread literacy in the Philippines. Numerous groups wrote their languages in related syllabaries whose origin traces back to India, with cognate scripts across Southeast Asia. Exactly how they reached the archipelago — directly from the mainland, or via Java, Borneo, Celebes, the Moluccas — is still debated.

A record of the Kapampangan syllabary survives thanks to Sinibaldo de Mas, a Spanish diplomat. Forman notes that many Kapampangans today are unaware it ever existed, though it survived much of the Spanish period.

Printing arrived early: the Spanish introduced it to the Pampanga area by 1610, and a Kapampangan named Antonio Bamba was printing with type in Bacolor by 1618.

💡 The script is not gone

The Kapampangan syllabary is today usually called kulitan, and it has been the focus of a serious revival — taught in workshops, used in signage and art, and a visible emblem of the language movement.

Forman was writing in 1971, when it looked close to forgotten. That it did not stay forgotten is part of the story lesson 21 tells.

3. The Spanish orthographies

The friars needed to preach in the local language, so they had to write it. Rather than learn the existing syllabary, they found it "convenient and simple" to write Kapampangan with Spanish conventions.

The key one to know:

SoundBefore a, o, uBefore e, i
/k/cqu
/g/ggu

So modern keni was written queni, modern Kapampangan was Capampangan, and the town now written Wawa kept its colonial spelling Guagua.

These conventions hardened into tradition through catechisms, then literary, academic and news writing, and were fully established by the time the Americans arrived.

4. The Tagalog-based revision

The third system replaces c and qu with k throughout, and generally aligns Kapampangan spelling with the national language's conventions. It is what this course uses and what you will meet in most modern material.

5. Reading real signage

Forman collected public signs in Pampanga. They are in the older orthography, and they are the best reading practice in this lesson.

In the street

Bawal ing mugse basura queni. Multa P5 o suculan.
Throwing garbage here is prohibited. 5 peso fine or jail.

Bawal ing mimi queti.
Urinating here is forbidden.

At church

E co misusulud imalan a "sleeveless" qng pamakinabang.
Don't wear sleeveless dresses to Communion.

On a vehicle

Distancia koy. — Keep your distance, brother.

Pot-pot bayo lusot. — Toot-toot before you cut in.

Work through the church sign with what you know. E is the negator; co is ko, "you (plural)"; misusulud is a mi- form of sulud ("wear") with progressive reduplication; imalan is "clothing," which you met in lesson 6; a is the linker; and qng is king. The English loanword sits in quotation marks in the original.

And Pot-pot bayo lusot uses bayu, "before," from lesson 15's relator list.

6. Why people care

🏛️ "The real Kapampangan"

Forman warns the learner directly: "some Kapampangans have very strong opinions about how the 'real' Kapampangan is supposed to be written."

This is not pedantry. An orthography carries a claim about what a language is and whose it is. Spelling Kapampangan by Tagalog rules can be read as accepting Tagalog's authority over it; spelling it the Spanish way can be read as clinging to a colonial convention; reviving kulitan is an argument that Kapampangan had its own literate tradition before either.

As a learner you do not have to take a side. You do have to know that a side is being taken, and not be surprised when a correction to your spelling turns out to be about something larger.

  • "Pot-pot bayo lusot" uses bayu meaning ___ (Answer: before)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Should I learn kulitan? Not for comprehension — almost nothing practical is written in it. For connection to the language movement, many learners find it worthwhile.

Which spelling should I write in? The modern one, unless the people you are writing for prefer otherwise. Then follow them.

Why do place names keep old spellings? Because they were registered officially and never changed. Guagua is the legal name of the town.

Why This Lesson Matters

Knowing the Spanish conventions roughly doubles what you can read — every old prayer book, sign, gravestone and family document becomes accessible with one substitution rule.
