1. Why we come back to these

Beginner lesson 6 taught four demonstratives and four locatives — enough to point at things. That was a simplification of a chart with four distances across five columns, most of them inflecting for number.

You also need these because they turn up constantly inside the noun phrases of lessons 10 and 16 — iting taung a iti, deng marakal a taung a ren.

Five distinct forms per distance, most with a plural. Forman also notes dialect variants awti, awni, awta, awyan, areti, areni, areta, aryan.

3. The case forms

This is the part beginner lesson 6 left out entirely. A demonstrative takes the same three cases a noun does.

CaseNear meNear usMeaning

You have already met these without noticing. In lesson 16's kanitang minunang panaun, kanita is the king-case form of ita. In lesson 14, Balu mu kanu… and in beginner lesson 8, kanyan was glossed as "now" — Forman notes that use explicitly.

4. The presentatives

A separate column, used for here it is and there it is — pointing something out rather than referring to it.

SingularPluralSense
oitioretihere it is (by me)
oiniorenihere it is (by us)
oitaoretathere it is (by you)
oyanoryanthere it is (yonder)

Oita ing bale ra. — Their house is that one there.

Oreni na ring tiket. — These are the tickets here now.

Oini ing kapatad ku. — This one here is my sibling.

Oita ya pala ing salamin mu! — So that's where your glasses are!

That last one packs the realisation particle pala from lesson 14 into a presentative — "oh, so that's where it is." Exactly the moment the particle exists for.

5. Indefinite substitutes

Built from the question words, with man or reduplication.

FormMeaningBuilt from
ninumanwhosoever, anyoneninu + man
ninuninueverybodyninu, reduplicated
nanumanwhatever, anythingnanu + man
nanunanuall sorts of thingsnanu, reduplicated

The pattern is worth generalising: question word + man gives "-ever"; question word doubled gives a collective or a variety.

You saw maskinanu in lesson 13 — maski + nanu, "no matter what" — in e la mamangan maskinanu, "they didn't eat anything."

6. Variation and honesty

⚠️ The disagreement the beginner course flagged

Beginner lesson 6 carries a callout saying that sources disagree about which demonstrative sits in which distance slot, and that this course follows Forman. The chart above is that decision, laid out in full.

Other descriptions — including the English Wikipedia article — put ini nearest the speaker and ita furthest away, close to the reverse of Forman on two of the four.

Forman himself writes that the variation between keta, ken and kyan "is not yet well understood," and suspects it parallels the pronoun portmanteauing that happens in the presence of lexical particles. Which is to say: even the reference grammar has an open question here.

Follow the speakers around you. A four-way distance contrast is certain; the exact labelling is the part that varies.

Exercise 3: Presentatives

  • "here it is (by us)" → ___ (Answer: oini)
  • "these are here now" → ___ (Answer: oreni)

Exercise 4: Indefinites

  • "whosoever" → ___ (Answer: ninuman)
  • "whatever" → ___ (Answer: nanuman)
  • "everybody" → ___ (Answer: ninuninu)
  • Question word plus ___ gives the "-ever" sense (Answer: man)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I really need all twenty-odd forms? To produce, no. To read, yes — the case forms especially, because kanita and niti look nothing like ita and iti on the page.

Why does kanyan mean "now"? A distance-in-space form pressed into service for distance-in-time. English does the same with "then."

Which arrangement should I use when speaking? Whichever the people you speak with use. If you have no speakers yet, use this chart and stay ready to change.

Why This Lesson Matters

Deictics are the highest-frequency closed class in any language after pronouns. The case forms in particular are unavoidable in text, and the indefinite substitutes give you "anyone," "anything" and "wherever" for free once you see the pattern.
