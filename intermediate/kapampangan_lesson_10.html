Attributive Phrases — Linked, Unlinked and Split

The linker at full depth, including the trick where an attribute leaves its head behind

1. Two devices, not one

Beginner lesson 15 taught the linker as an adjective rule. It is more than that. Forman describes two ways of stating an attributive relationship:

marked juxtaposition — with a or -ng
unmarked juxtaposition — with nothing at all

And a third device you already know: a ning-marked phrase modifying a noun head, which Forman calls "a very frequently occurring pattern."

2. Linked attributives

The rule is phonological, exactly as the beginner course said: a after a head word ending in a consonant, -ng after one ending in a vowel.

maganakang lalaki — a good man

masanting a bale — an attractive house

malagung dalaga — beautiful girl  [malagu + -ng + dalaga]

mayap a yabak — good morning  [mayap + a + abak]

mayap a gatpanapun — good afternoon

mayap a bengi — good evening

💡 Forman writes out the greeting

That bracketed derivation is his, not mine — he analyses mayap a yabak as mayap + a + abak, with the glottal stop of abak surfacing as y after the linker.

Lesson 5 predicted this from the sound rules. Here is the grammar confirming it in the very first phrase the beginner course taught you.

3. When both linkers appear

Occasionally you get -ng and a together:

iting taung a iti — this man right here

deng marakal a taung a ren — those many people there

Forman offers two explanations and commits to neither: it may be a feature of slow or self-corrected speech, or the a may not be a linker at all but part of the demonstrative pronoun. He writes that "it may turn out on further analysis" to be the latter.

Notice marakal in that second phrase — dakal with the d → r change from lesson 5, because ma- ends in a vowel.

4. Unlinked attributives

Some attributive pairs take no linker whatsoever:

asan dagat — deep-sea fish

kural damulag — carabao corral

lele dagat — edge of the sea

mula hangule — vegetable garden

ing bunduk Arayat — Mt. Arayat

king kasumangid mula — in the next yard

It is tempting to call these compound nouns and be done. Forman resists, because the same pattern appears in phrases no one would call compounds — aldo ning kebaitan "birthday," or that last locative example.

He also sets up a minimal contrast: dalagang bukid — linked, dalaga + -ng + bukid — is a kind of catfish, not a "mountain maiden." Linking or not linking can be the difference between a phrase and a name.

5. Splitting attribute from head

Here is the one that will trip you up in real speech. An attributive word can be separated from its head by a cluster of pronouns and particles.

Anam na ke banwa miasawa.
We've been married six years now.

WordRole
anam"six" — attributive to banwa
naparticle: now, already
kefused pronoun
banwa"year" — the head, two words away from its attribute
miasawa"married" — a mi- stative from lesson 6

Numbers usually take first position in these constructions. What normally follows — the head noun — is displaced by the pronoun-and-particle cluster, and the head arrives late.

🌱 Why this matters more than it looks

If you are parsing left to right and expecting anam to be followed by what there are six of, this sentence derails you immediately.

The habit to build: when you meet a number or descriptive followed by particles, hold it open and keep reading. Its head is coming.

6. Attributes that are whole sentences

A head word can be linked to an entire embedded sentence. When it is, the linker is a.

pialungan a e pupulai — a game with no running

The head is pialungan ("game"); a links it to the clause e pupulai ("not running"). English needs a preposition and a gerund; Kapampangan just links a clause.

kanitang minunang panaun a mabie ya pa
way back a long time ago when he was still alive

Forman unpacks this one himself: the head is panaun ("time"); kanita and minuna are a pronoun and a verbal descriptive linked with -ng; and what follows panaun is the embedded sentence mabie ya pa ("he is still alive"), attached by a.

This is the machinery behind relative clauses, which lesson 16 takes up properly.

7. Practice

Exercise 1: a or -ng?

  • masanting + bale → "masanting ___ bale" (Answer: a)
  • malagu + dalaga → "malagu___ dalaga" (Answer: ng)
  • mayap + bengi → "mayap ___ bengi" (Answer: a)

Exercise 2: Linked or unlinked?

  • asan dagat is ___ (Answer: unlinked)
  • dalagang bukid is ___ (Answer: linked)
  • lele dagat is ___ (Answer: unlinked)

Exercise 3: The split

In "Anam na ke banwa miasawa":

  • the attributive word is ___ (Answer: anam)
  • its head noun is ___ (Answer: banwa)
  • the particle between them is ___ (Answer: na)

Exercise 4: Complex attributives

  • When the attribute is a whole clause, the linker is ___ (Answer: a)
  • In "pialungan a e pupulai", the head word is ___ (Answer: pialungan)

Exercise 5: Sound rules in the phrase

  • In "marakal", the r comes from an underlying ___ (Answer: d)
  • In "mayap a yabak", the y comes from a ___ stop (Answer: glottal)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

When can I leave the linker out? Not at will — the unlinked pairs are fixed expressions. Use the linker unless you have heard otherwise.

Is splitting common? Common enough that you must expect it. It happens whenever a particle cluster wants the position right after the first word.

Why does Forman hedge so much here? Because attributive syntax is where the analysis is least settled. A grammar that stated a clean rule would be overstating the evidence.

Why This Lesson Matters

Noun phrases are where Kapampangan puts most of its complexity. Once you can read a split attributive and a clause-sized attribute, long sentences stop being walls.
