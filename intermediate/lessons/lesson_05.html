Lesson 4 handled the vowels. This lesson handles the consonants — and it is the one that pays off fastest, because these changes are what disguise familiar roots most thoroughly.

1. Nasal assimilation

When a prefix ending in a nasal meets a root, the root's first consonant and the nasal merge. Which nasal you end up with depends on where the consonant is made in the mouth.

This is why the prefix is written paN- and maN- with a capital N in grammars: the N is a placeholder for whichever nasal the following sound calls for.

3. d becomes r

d becomes r between vowels — after a prefix ending in a vowel, or after a preceding word that ends in one.

RootDerivedMeaning
dagul — bigdaragul (also dadagul)grow big
dakal — manymarakal (also madakal)many
dimla — coldmarimla (but: didimla)cold

💡 Why the markers come in pairs

The beginner course told you ding/ring and di/ri and da/ra were "free variation, both standard, pick one."

They are the same words, before and after this rule. The r-forms appear where the preceding sound is a vowel. That is not two vocabulary items; it is one item and a sound change.

Note too that marimla "cold" — which you learned in beginner lesson 15 as a flat vocabulary item — is ma- + dimla with d→r applied.

Forman is careful here: the change is not automatic. Not every VrV sequence comes from an underlying d, and some words resist it — dudulduL "it is thundering," Maria. In a few items the two vary freely, like kumbida / kumbira "invite."

4. The glottal stop becomes y or w

Beginner lesson 1 mentioned the glottal stop and said Kapampangan spelling does not mark it. Here is where it becomes visible.

A root with an initial glottal stop turns that stop into y after a prefix ending in a vowel, or inside an attribute phrase joined by the linker a. A root with a final glottal stop turns it into w before a suffix.

RootChanged

💡 "Mayap a abak" or "Mayap a yabak"?

The very first thing the beginner course taught you was the greeting Mayap a abak, with a note that you would also hear Mayap a yabak and that "the y is a glide that grows naturally between the two vowels."

That was right, and this is the rule: abak begins with a glottal stop, and after the linker a that stop surfaces as y. Forman lists this exact example.

Your first Kapampangan sentence contained a morphophonemic rule you are only now in a position to see.

5. Doubled consonants at word boundaries

When an enclitic begins with the same sound the preceding word ends with, the two do not both get pronounced. Instead the preceding vowel lengthens and you hear one consonant.

Written asSaid as
kayapan + nakayapa·na
anak + keana·ke
dakal + ladaka·la
kinasal + lakinasa·la

This is a pronunciation rule rather than a spelling one, and it is a large part of why fluent Kapampangan sounds faster than it looks on the page.

6. A method for unknown words

Put lessons 4 and 5 together and you have a procedure. When you meet a form you do not know:

  1. Strip any prefix you recognise — ma-, mag-, maN-, paN-, pi-, ka-, mi-, i-.
  2. Strip -an if it is there.
  3. Undo y → s or d if the form has ny.
  4. Undo r → d if there is an r between vowels.
  5. Undo i → u and e → a.
  6. Restore a lost vowel or turn a glide back into one.

marakal → strip ma- → rakal → undo r→d → dakal, "many". ✔

panyanuman → strip pan- and -an → yanum → undo y→d → danum, "water". ✔

kimbiran → keep -an → undo i→u → kumbira, "invite". ✔

7. Practice

Exercise 1: Assimilation

Which nasal appears?

  • before p or b you get ___ (Answer: m)
  • before t or d you get ___ (Answer: n)
  • before k or g you get ___ (Answer: ng)

Exercise 2: Apply the rules

  • maN- + balbas → ___ (Answer: mamalbas)
  • maN- + kutang → ___ (Answer: mangutang)
  • paN- + damdam → ___ (Answer: panamdam)
  • pang- + danum + -an → ___ (Answer: panyanuman)

Exercise 3: Work backwards to the root

  • marakal comes from ___ (Answer: dakal)
  • marimla comes from ___ (Answer: dimla)
  • manyalita comes from ___ (Answer: salita)
  • manyakap comes from ___ (Answer: dakap)

Exercise 4: The greeting

  • In "mayap a yabak", the y is an underlying ___ stop (Answer: glottal)
  • The root there is ___ (Answer: abak)

Exercise 5: Geminates

  • dakal + la is pronounced ___ (Answer: dakala)
  • anak + ke is pronounced ___ (Answer: anake)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Do I need to apply these when I speak? Gradually. Native speakers apply them without thinking. Learners usually get assimilation right by imitation long before they could state the rule.

What if a word resists the rule? Some do — Forman names Maria and dudulduL. These are tendencies with exceptions, not algorithms.

Is this the hardest lesson in B1? It is the densest. It is also the one with the highest return, because it unlocks the reading of forms nobody has taught you.

Why This Lesson Matters

These two lessons are the difference between a learner who needs every word taught and one who can work out a form from its parts. Everything after this assumes you can find a root.
