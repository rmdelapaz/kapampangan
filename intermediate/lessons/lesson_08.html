1. A trap worth naming first

⚠️ maka- has more than one job, and the sources split them

In Tagalog, maka- is the potentive: makakita "be able to see." A learner naturally carries that across.

Forman's grammar describes Kapampangan maka- as a stative descriptive — something causes a state or is in one. Makatamad ya ing aldong ngeni is "the day today makes one lazy," not "the day can be lazy."

But Bergaño's 1736 grammar devotes six sections to maca- and records a genuine potential sense alongside the others: macasúlat co, "I can write."

So the honest statement is: maka- is polysemous. The stative descriptive is what you will meet most in the everyday examples Forman collected; the potential is real and documented. Read from context, not from the prefix.

Bergaño calls it "the most ambiguous and difficult composition in the whole language," and says he spent close to ten years on it. If it does not resolve cleanly for you, you are in good company.

This is the same class of trap as susulat in beginner lesson 11: a form that exists in both languages and means different things. Shared shape, different grammar.

2. maka- descriptives

The commonest sense: X makes one Y.

Makatamad ya ing aldong ngeni. — The day today makes one lazy.

Makaputla ing aslam. — Vinegar makes you pale.

Makasora ing sinabi na kanaku. — What he said to me is annoying.

Makakayap ing bubulasan. — Being scolded makes one good.

Makasanting ing mibablas mung mibablas.
Always changing your clothes makes you look nice.

Makabawal ing pamipalimbag alang kapaintulutan.
Printing without permission is prohibited.

Compare these with the plain ma- adjectives from beginner lesson 15:

ma- adjectivemaka- descriptive
matamad — lazymakatamad — makes one lazy
masanting — beautifulmakasanting — makes one look nice
mayap — goodmakakayap — makes one good

ma- describes the thing. maka- describes what the thing does to you.

3. The "in a state" sense

The other use is closer to a resultant condition — something is in the state named by the root.

Makasalikut nongan. — They're all hidden.

Nanu la retang makabalut papil? — What are those things wrapped in paper?

Malinis at makasalilung ing karin. — The place there is clean and shady.

Makatulay ya king susulud na. — He looks funny because of what he's wearing.

Kanyaman ning makaduyan! — How delightful to be in a hammock!

And a very high-frequency one you should simply learn whole:

makanyan — "like that"
Maglakad lang makanyan. — They walk like that.
E me ipusit makanyan. — Don't squeeze it like that.

4. The a- ability forms

So where did ability go? Onto a bare a- prefix.

Magkanu ing akakit mu king kilub ning aldo-aldo?
How much are you able to earn in the course of a day?

Abili ke pin pala king bulsa ku.
Oh yeah — I guess I happened to put it in my pocket.

Akua keng danum i Tatang. — I was able to get water for Father.

Kutnan me i Nene nung malyari ne ikua. — Ask Nene if she can catch it.

Notice that the a- forms cover both meanings English splits:

FormReading
akakitcan see / is able to see / manages to see
abilihappened to put — the accidental reading
akuawas able to get

"Was able to" and "happened to" are closer than they look: both say the event occurred without the doer fully controlling it. Kapampangan treats that as one idea.

💡 You have seen this already

Beginner lesson 5 taught Akakit me? — "Do you see him?" It was presented as a vocabulary item.

It is a- + kakit + the fused pronoun me. Literally closer to "are you able to see him?" — which is, in fact, how English often asks it too ("can you see him?").

5. malyari, the analytic option

Alongside the affixed forms, Kapampangan has a free-standing word for possibility — the one beginner lesson 16 gave you.

Malyari ku waring lumwal? — May I go out?

Malyari ne ikua. — She can catch it.

For producing speech, malyari plus a verb will carry almost any ability or permission meaning you need. The affixed forms are what you must be able to hear.

💡 sucat — the distinction Bergaño draws

You met sukat in beginner lesson 16 as "ought to." Bergaño treats it as a potential too, and draws a distinction English does not:

  • maca- expresses only proximate ability — what you could do right now.
  • sukat covers both proximate and remote ability — what you are in principle capable of.

His example: of someone who cannot write, you cannot say he "could" with maca-, because that is remote ability — but you can with sukat. And of a man in shackles:

Sukat yang lumakad, dapot ali ya makalakad.
He is able to walk, but he cannot walk.

That sentence is not a contradiction. It says: walking is within his powers, but not available to him now.

🌱 This lesson has been wrong twice

It was first drafted as "the potentive maka-," straight from Tagalog. Reading Forman showed his examples are all stative descriptives, so it was rewritten to say flatly that maka- is not the potentive.

Then Bergaño turned up with macasúlat co, "I can write," and a whole section headed MACA potencial. The confident correction was itself an overstatement — built on treating one grammar's coverage as the whole picture.

Both errors have the same shape: deciding a question from the source in front of me rather than from the sources that exist. Worth watching for, because you will do it too.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

So can I use maka- the Tagalog way? Sometimes, and Bergaño would back you. But Forman's everyday data is overwhelmingly stative, so expect the descriptive reading first and let context argue you out of it.

Is a- related to the maka- of other Philippine languages? Historically these affixes are connected across the family; the point is that their modern functions have diverged. Do not reason from cognates.

How do I say "I can't"? E ku malyari, or negate the a- form: e ku akakit, "I can't see it."

Why This Lesson Matters

maka- descriptives are everywhere in ordinary talk about food, weather, work and people. Reading them as potentives produces sentences that seem almost to make sense — the most dangerous kind of misunderstanding.
