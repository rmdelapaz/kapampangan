Lesson 1 put the thing in focus. This lesson puts the person it was done for in focus — and because it shares the i- prefix with goal focus, it is the commonest thing to confuse with it.

1. The shape of a beneficiary predication

Forman identifies it by a cluster of features rather than one affix:

a verb prefixed with i-, paN- or pag-
+ two noun phrases referring to persons
+ optionally a referential object, unmarked or marked with ning

The ing-marked phrase is the beneficiary.

A king-marked phrase may also appear; if it does, it usually means location.

Ikua meng danum i Lus. — Get some water for Lus.

Kingua neng danum ning anak i Inang. — A child got water for Mother.

Look at that second sentence carefully. Danum ("water") is the thing being got, but it is not in focus — it sits unmarked. The child, the agent, takes ning. And i Inang, the one it is for, is the focused phrase.

2. i- forms

Igawa mu kung baru. — Make a dress for me.

Gewa neng baru i Inang na. — She made a dress for her mother.

Isali mu kung tinape. — Buy some bread for me.

Isali meng baru i Kristi. — Buy a dress for Christie.

Seli neng baru i Kristi. — He bought a dress for Christie.

💡 Compare with lesson 1

In lesson 1 you met Seli ku ini — "I bought this," with the thing in focus.

Here the same completed form appears in Seli neng baru i Kristi — "He bought a dress for Christie," with the person in focus and the dress demoted to an unmarked object.

Same verb form. Different predication. The noun phrases tell you which.

3. paN- forms

The paN- prefix is very productive here — and it is where you first see the assimilation that lesson 5 will formalise.

Panyad mu kung abyas keng siping tang bale.
Ask our neighbours for some rice for me.

Panyakap neng Kitong tulang ing wali na.
Kitong is catching a dragonfly for his younger brother.

Pinyakap neng Kitong tulang ing wali na.
Kitong caught a dragonfly for his younger brother.

Panyali na kang diariu ning lalaki.
The man is buying a newspaper for you.

Pinyali da kaming libro.
They bought books for us.

Pandaman mu kung sukle, Nena.
Lend me a comb, Nena.

💡 Where did the s go?

Panyali comes from paN- + sali ("buy"). The s has not vanished — the prefix's nasal and the root's s have merged into ny.

The same thing happens in panyakap (from sakap) and panyawad (from sawad). This is not irregularity; it is a rule, and lesson 5 states it.

Note also the completed forms: paN- becomes piN- or peN-. Panyali → pinyali; panyad → penyad.

4. pag- forms

A smaller set uses pag-:

Pagdala nong sampagang Pedritu ding maistra.
Pedrito will bring flowers for the teachers.

Pegtimplan neng kape.
Someone made coffee for him.

And again the vowel change: pag- → peg- in the completed form, exactly parallel to mag- → meg- from beginner lesson 11.

5. Telling beneficiary from goal

There is no single reliable affix test. What there is:

AskGoal focusBeneficiary focus
What is ing-marked?the thing acted ona person
How many person phrases?usually onetwo
Where is the object?in focusunmarked, or ning-marked
Typical prefixes-an, i-i-, paN-, pag-

Isali meng baru i Kristi. — two people (you, Christie), dress unmarked → beneficiary.

Isara me ing pasbul. — one person, door in focus → goal.

🌱 On living with ambiguity

Lesson 1 quoted Forman admitting that the classification of verb roots here is unfinished. That applies doubly to this distinction.

You are not going to reach a state where every sentence parses cleanly into a labelled box, because the grammar itself has not reached that state. What you are building is a reliable reading — and that comes from the noun phrases, not from the affix.

6. Reduced beneficiary sentences

Either of the two central noun phrases can be dropped when context makes it obvious — or absorbed into a portmanteau pronoun.

Akua keng danum i Tatang. — I was able to get water for Father.

Ipabusbus me balugbug i Julieta. — Pierce Julieta's ears for her.

That ke in the first sentence is ku + ya doing double duty. The fused pronouns are not a beginner's curiosity — they are load-bearing at every level of this language.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Can I just use para king instead? For producing speech, often yes — an analytic "for X" phrase is always available and will be understood. For understanding, no: the affixed constructions are what people actually say.

Why does the same form mean two things? Because the affix marks that something is in focus, not which role it plays. The roles are recovered from the noun phrases.

Is paN- here the same paN- as the instrument prefix? They are related and sometimes hard to separate — which is lesson 3's subject.

Why This Lesson Matters

Doing things for other people is a large part of what anyone talks about, and Kapampangan gives it a dedicated construction rather than a preposition. Reading it correctly is the difference between "he bought a dress" and "he bought a dress for Christie."
