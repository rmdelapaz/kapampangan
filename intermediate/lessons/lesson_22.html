1. Beyond the sentence

Forman's grammar essentially stops at the sentence, and he says so — his concluding chapter is four pages. That is an honest limit of a 100-page reference grammar, and it means this last lesson leans more on general principle and less on one source than the rest of the tier.

What it can do is assemble the machinery you already have into the shapes that extended speech needs.

2. Signposting

The relators from lesson 15 are what tell a listener where you are.

To signalUse
addingat, at saka, ampo
contrastingdapot, oneng, pero
explaininguling, kasi
concedingagyang, maski
sequencingbayu, kabang, kaybat
concludinginya, kaya

Lesson 15's three-clause example is the model:

Buri ra kang paglapakan oneng e ra ka agyu uling maragul ka.
They'd like to beat you up, but they can't overcome you because you're big.

Claim, concession, reason — three clauses, two relators. If you can produce that shape reliably, you can hold a turn.

3. Shaping a narrative

Three things carry a Kapampangan narrative:

Time words. Because the verb marks aspect rather than tense (beginner lesson 11), the time words do more work than in English: ngeni, napun, nabengi, kanita, neng misan ("once, sometimes"), misan a yaldo ("one day").

I Suan, pintalan ne misan a yaldo i Pedro dapot e ne disan.
Suan went to Pedro one day, but didn't get to him.

Aspect contrast. Completed forms carry the events; progressive forms paint the background; the recent-completion construction of lesson 7 marks something that has just landed.

The particles. Lesson 14's inventory is what gives narrative its texture — kanu for what you were told rather than saw, pala for the twist, rugu for sympathy with someone in the story.

💡 kanu is the storyteller's particle

A narrative told with kanu running through it is explicitly second-hand — a story you are passing on, not one you witnessed.

English speakers signal this once ("apparently…") and then drop it. Kapampangan can keep it marked throughout, which is why folk narrative sounds the way it does.

4. Backchannelling

Listening audibly is half of conversation. Lesson 17's minor sentences and lesson 14's particles are the toolkit:

Wa. · Wa pin. · Wa pala. · Wa rugu. · Aru! · Naku! · Ala!

Each does something different. Wa pin affirms strongly; wa pala says "oh, I see — I hadn't realised"; wa rugu adds sympathy. A learner who varies these sounds engaged; one who says wa to everything sounds like a machine.

5. Taking stock

Over twenty-two lessons you have worked through:

  • the complete predication system — actor, goal, beneficiary, locative, instrumental, reason, stative, recent-completion
  • the morphophonemics that let you recover a root from a form you have never seen
  • attributive syntax, including splitting and clause-sized attributes
  • verb chaining and the pseudo-verbs
  • comparison in all three constructions
  • the four non-verbal predication types
  • the lexical particles and their ordering
  • coordination, subordination, embedding and relative clauses
  • minor sentences
  • the full deictic chart with its cases
  • three writing systems and a literature
  • dialect variation and the sociolinguistic situation

That is, roughly, the content of a reference grammar — worked through as lessons with the examples kept attached to their source.

🌱 The honest closing note

This tier was built from one reference grammar describing one town over six months in 1969, supplemented where it was silent and flagged where it was uncertain. That is a real foundation and a real limit.

Several times these lessons had to say "Forman does not know," "sources disagree," or "this was drafted wrong and corrected." Treat that as the model. The most useful thing you can carry forward is not the paradigms — it is the habit of checking, and of saying plainly which parts you are sure of.

Everything after this comes from listening.
