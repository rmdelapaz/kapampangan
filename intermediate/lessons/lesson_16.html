1. The linker as relativiser

Kapampangan has no dedicated relative pronoun — no equivalent of "who," "which" or "that." It uses the linker you have had since beginner lesson 15.

head noun  +  a / -ng  +  a whole clause

Lesson 10 established this: when a head word is linked to an embedded sentence, the linker is a. That is all a relative clause is.

2. Reading relatives

ing makisese kaku — the one who takes care of me

ing mete nang asu — his dog that died

pialungan a e pupulai — a game with no running

ing lalaking ita — that man

ing anak a ian — that child

ing lugar a pikuanan nang Maria ng ulunan
the place where Maria got the pillow

That last one is a full locative relative: the head is lugar ("place"), the linker is a, and what follows is the pi- place-form from lesson 3 with its own agent and object inside it.

💡 ing makisese kaku

Worth taking apart. maki- + sese ("tend, care for") gives "one who joins in caring"; kaku is "for me." And the whole thing is wrapped in ing, which turns the clause into a noun phrase.

Kapampangan builds "the one who takes care of me" out of four morphemes and no relative pronoun at all.

3. Stacking attributes

More than one attribute can hang on a single head. Forman's example:

kanitang minunang panaun a mabie ya pa
way back a long time ago when he was still alive

PieceRole
kanitaa demonstrative in the king case
-nglinker
minunaverbal descriptive: "earlier, former"
-nglinker
panaunthe head noun: "time"
alinker to a clause
mabie ya paembedded sentence: "he is still alive"

Two linkers before the head, one after it. The head noun sits in the middle of its own phrase, which is exactly what makes long Kapampangan noun phrases hard to parse on first encounter.

4. Unpacking a long noun phrase

A working procedure, built from lessons 10, 15 and this one:

  1. Find the marker — ing, ning, king — that opens the phrase.
  2. Find the head noun. It is the substantive, not the descriptive. It may not be first.
  3. Treat every linker as a seam. What is before it modifies what is after, or vice versa.
  4. Expect particle clusters in the second position, splitting attribute from head (lesson 14).
  5. Expect a clause after an a.

deng marakal a taung a ren
→ marker deng · head tau · attribute marakal · demonstrative a ren
those many people there

ing anak nang dalaga
→ marker ing · head anak · possessor nang dalaga
his daughter (literally "the child of the young woman")

🌱 Reading is a skill you can drill

Nobody parses like this in real time forever. But doing it deliberately, on paper, for a few dozen phrases is what makes it automatic later.

Take any Forman example in this tier, cover the translation, and work through the five steps. That exercise is worth more than another vocabulary list.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

How do I know if a is a linker or the personal marker? The personal marker is i, not a. A bare a between two content words is a linker.

Can relatives stack? Yes, and they do in written Kapampangan. Spoken Kapampangan tends to break them into separate clauses.

Is there a "which/that" distinction? No. The linker covers all of it, and context does the rest.

Why This Lesson Matters

Relative clauses are how you specify which one you mean, and they are unavoidable in written Kapampangan. This is also the lesson where the linker finally shows its full range — adjective, number, clause, complement and relative, all one morpheme.
