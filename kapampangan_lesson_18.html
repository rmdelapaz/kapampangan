King Palengke — Shopping

A whole market transaction, using six earlier lessons at once

📑 In This Lesson

  2. Asking the price
  3. Bargaining

  6. A full transaction

This lesson has almost no new grammar. That is the point: it is where you discover that lessons 8, 14, 15, 16 and 17 combine into something you can actually use. The palengke is the natural first test of a Philippine language, because the exchanges are short, predictable and repeated.

1. The market

KapampanganEnglish
palengkemarket
tindahanshop, store
tinderafemale vendor
salito buy
pisalito sell
bayadpayment; to pay
suklichange (money returned)
kwaltamoney

🏛️ Address the vendor properly

Lesson 7's kinship terms are the right tools here. A vendor a little older than you is atsi or koya; a much older one gets pu and deference. Using a kinship term rather than nothing at all is what marks a regular customer from a tourist.

2. Asking the price

Magkanu ya ini?
How much is this?

Three lessons meet in that sentence: magkanu from lesson 14, ya from lesson 4, ini from lesson 6.

Magkanu ya ing metung a tinape? — How much is one bread?

Magkanu la reti? — How much are these?

And the two reactions you will need:

Mal ya.It's expensive.
Mura ya.It's cheap.

3. Bargaining

Tawad is a discount, and asking for one is expected at a market stall (though not in a supermarket or a restaurant).

Pakitawad naman. — Could you lower it a bit, please.

Mal ya naman pu. — That's a bit expensive, sir/ma'am.

Ala kung kwalta. — I have no money. (said with a smile)

💡 Bargaining is friendly, not adversarial

Haggling at a Philippine market is a social exchange with a light tone, not a confrontation. The softening particles from lesson 17 do the real work: naman and pu turn what could sound like a complaint into a pleasant negotiation.

Push gently, accept the answer gracefully, and buy something anyway. Grinding a vendor down over a few pesos reads badly — especially from a foreigner.

4. Quantities and change

KapampanganEnglish
pilan a…how many… (+ noun)
kilokilo
dakeportion, share
sablanall, everything

Quantities use lesson 8's numerals plus lesson 15's linker:

Aduang kilo — two kilos (adua ends in a vowel → -ng)

Limang piso — five pesos

Metung a tinape — one bread (metung ends in a consonant → a)

And to settle up:

Ini ing bayad ku. — Here's my payment.

Nukarin ya ing sukli ku? — Where is my change?

6. A full transaction

Ika: Mayap a abak pu!
Good morning!

Tindera: Mayap a abak. Nanu ya ing buri mu?
Good morning. What would you like?

Ika: Magkanu la reng saging?
How much are the bananas?

Tindera: Limang piso ya ing metung.
Five pesos each.

Ika: Mal ya naman pu. Pakitawad naman.
That's a bit expensive. Could you lower it a little?

Tindera: Sige, apat a piso.
All right, four pesos.

Ika: Salamat pu. Bisa kung lima.
Thank you. I'd like five.

Tindera: Aduang pulu a piso sablan.
Twenty pesos altogether.

Ika: Ini ing bayad ku. Dakal a salamat pu!
Here's my payment. Thank you very much!

Read it again and count the lessons: greetings (2), markers (3), pronouns (4), demonstratives (6), numbers (8), questions (14), the linker (15), bisa (16), politeness particles (17). Nothing in that dialogue is new.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Will vendors answer me in Kapampangan? In Pampanga's town markets, often yes — and often faster than you can follow. Asking Makananu? or simply looking baffled will get a slower repeat.

Is haggling always appropriate? At market stalls and with street vendors, yes. In shops with fixed price tags, no. Read the setting.

What if I run out of words mid-transaction? E ku balu (lesson 13) and pointing will carry you. Nobody expects fluency, and the attempt is the part people respond to.

Why This Lesson Matters

The palengke is where a language stops being a study project. The exchanges are short enough to survive, repetitive enough to improve at, and frequent enough to practise daily. Most people's first genuinely successful conversation in a Philippine language happens over produce.
