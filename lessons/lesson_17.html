Kapampangan does not have one "polite form" you switch on. It has a set of small particles you layer, and the amount you layer signals how much deference the moment calls for. Think of it as a dial rather than a switch.

1. The plain command

The simplest imperative is the verb plus ka ("you").

Mangan ka. — Eat.

Ume ka keni. — Come here.

Lukluk ka. — Sit down.

Unadorned, these are direct — fine between close friends or from an elder to a child, blunt in most other situations. You almost never want the bare form when speaking to someone you have just met.

2. Commands with fused pronouns

When the command is about doing something to something, the fused pronouns from lesson 5 appear.

Sabian me. — Say it. / Tell it.

Sabian me kaku. — Tell it to me.

Me keni. — Come here. (short form of Ume ka keni)

Me in Sabian me is mu + ya — "you … it". Note that Me keni is a different me: a contraction of ume ka. Context separates them cleanly in practice.

ParticleWhat it does
puthe core respect marker — use with elders, strangers, anyone serving you
namansoftens a request; roughly "do…", "won't you", "please"
pinsoftens an imperative and adds affirmation; "indeed"
pa"still, else" — often softens by making the request sound small

🏛️ When in doubt, add pu

As a learner you will misjudge the register constantly. The safe error is being too polite — that reads as respectful and slightly formal, which is a fine thing for a foreigner to be. Being too blunt reads as rude, and people will not tell you.

So: pu by default. Drop it when someone your own age drops it first.

Also useful for smoothing an interaction:

KapampanganEnglish
Pasensya na pu.Sorry / excuse me (polite)
Salamat pu.Thank you (polite)

4. Asking permission

Rather than commanding, it is often better to ask. Lesson 16's malyari plus lesson 14's wari does exactly this.

Malyari ku waring lumwal? — May I go out?

Malyari ku wari…? — May I…?

You can also frame a request as a question about the other person's willingness, which is gentler still:

Malyari mu wari…? — Could you…?

5. Let's — tana

Tana is the inclusive "let us", built on tamu from lesson 4. It explicitly includes the person you are talking to — which is the whole point of a suggestion.

Mangan tana! — Let's eat!

Ume tana. — Let's go.

A suggestion is socially very different from a command: it puts you and your listener on the same side of the action. In a culture that places real weight on group belonging, reaching for tana instead of ka is often the better move.

6. Saying don't

Negative commands use the negator from lesson 13 in front of the imperative.

E ka mangan. — Don't eat.

Edaka buring mawala! — I don't want to lose you!

Negative commands are blunt by nature, so the softeners matter even more here. E ka pu… takes a considerable edge off.

7. Practice

Exercise 1: Give the command

  • "Eat." → "Mangan ___." (Answer: ka)
  • "Sit down." → "Lukluk ___." (Answer: ka)
  • "Come here." → "Ume ka ___." (Answer: keni)

Exercise 2: Turn up the politeness

  • Make "Mangan ka" respectful: "Mangan ka ___." (Answer: pu)
  • Soften a request with: ___ (Answer: naman)
  • "Sorry (polite)" → "Pasensya na ___." (Answer: pu)

Exercise 3: Let's

  • "Let's eat!" → "Mangan ___!" (Answer: tana)
  • "Let's go." → "Ume ___." (Answer: tana)

Exercise 4: Permission and prohibition

  • "May I go out?" → "___ ku waring lumwal?" (Answer: malyari)
  • "Don't eat." → "___ ka mangan." (Answer: e|ali)

Exercise 5: Fused pronouns in commands

  • "Tell it to me." → "Sabian ___ kaku." (Answer: me)
  • In "Sabian me", the me is made of ___ (Answer: mu ya)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Can I really put pu on everything? Very nearly. It is hard to overuse in a way that causes offence — the worst outcome is sounding formal.

Is a bare imperative always rude? No. Between close friends, from parents to children, and in urgent situations it is perfectly normal. The rudeness comes from mismatching the register to the relationship.

What about paki-? Tagalog's paki- prefix for "please do" is widely understood in Pampanga and used in Taglish speech. Native Kapampangan leans on pu, naman and pin instead.

Why This Lesson Matters

Politeness is where foreigners are judged most quickly and most quietly. Nobody will correct your aspect marking, but everyone registers whether you said pu. This lesson is short on grammar and long on consequences.
