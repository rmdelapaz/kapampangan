Kapampangan adjectives are unusually easy, because most of them are built with a single prefix. The interesting part of this lesson is not the adjectives — it is the two-letter word that joins them to nouns.

1. The ma- adjectives

Attach ma- to a root and you get a descriptive word. Once you spot the pattern, a large part of the adjective vocabulary becomes predictable.

KapampanganEnglish
mayapgood
marokbad
masantingbeautiful, fine, excellent
malagubeautiful (of a person)
maragulbig
malatismall
mapalihot
marimlacold
masakitpainful; difficult
matulahappy
mabilisfast
maniamandelicious
mabanglufragrant

Two common adjectives do not take ma-: matua ("old") does, but bayu ("new") does not, and neither does mal ("expensive"). The pattern is productive, not universal.

2. The linker

To attach an adjective to a noun, Kapampangan needs a linker. It has two shapes, and which one you use depends on the last sound of the preceding word.

Preceding word ends in…LinkerExample
a consonanta (separate word)masanting a bale — a beautiful house
a vowel-ng (attached)malagung babai — a beautiful woman

masanting a bale — a beautiful house (masanting ends in -ng, a consonant)

maragul a asu — a big dog (maragul ends in l)

malagung babai — a beautiful woman (malagu ends in u)

metung a bale — one house

aduang pulu — twenty (adua ends in a vowel)

💡 You have been using this since lesson 2

Mayap a abak — "good morning" — is an adjective linked to a noun. So is dakal a salamat, "many thanks". So is metung a kapatad, "one sibling".

The linker has been in every lesson. Now you know why it is there.

The linker also works in the other direction — noun first, adjective second — and both orders are used:

ing lalaking ita — that man (lalaki + -ng + ita)

ing anak a ian — that child

3. masanting — the flagship word

masanting

beautiful · fine · excellent · well-made · good

If Kapampangan has a signature adjective, this is it. It stretches across a range that English splits between half a dozen words, and Kapampangans use it constantly — of a house, a meal, a plan, a performance, a day.

Masanting ya ing bale. — The house is beautiful.

Masanting a bie. — A good life.

Note there is no word for "is" in that first sentence — just masanting + ya + ing bale. Lesson 10 warned you about this; here it is in ordinary use.

Notice that three of the five take ma-, behaving like ordinary adjectives. Berde is a Spanish borrowing, as it is in most Philippine languages.

5. ka- — turning an adjective into a thing

Swap ma- for ka- and the adjective becomes the quality itself — an abstract noun.

AdjectiveQuality noun
malagu — beautifulkalagu — beauty
maragul — bigkaragul — size, bigness

Nuanti ka kalagu? — How beautiful you are!
Literally closer to "to what degree are you [in] beauty?"

Nuanti ya karagul? — How big is it?

This is why nuanti from lesson 14 pairs with ka- forms: it asks about a degree, and ka- names the quality being measured.

6. Making a superlative

Prefix peka- to make "most".

pekamasanting — most beautiful

pekamaragul — biggest

For comparisons, everyday Kapampangan borrows Spanish mas, as Tagalog does:

mas maragul — bigger

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is the linker really necessary? Yes. Leaving it out makes a phrase ungrammatical in the way "a beautiful the house" is in English — understandable but visibly wrong.

Do I write -ng joined or separate? Joined, to the word that ends in the vowel: malagung babai, not malagu ng babai.

Can I use masanting for everything? Nearly. It is genuinely broad, and overusing it is a very Kapampangan habit rather than a learner's error.

Why This Lesson Matters

The linker is not an adjective rule — it is a general-purpose connector that joins numbers to nouns, demonstratives to nouns, and clauses to each other. You have been using it since your first greeting. Understanding it retroactively clarifies a dozen phrases you had been learning as unanalysed blocks.
