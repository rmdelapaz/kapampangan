You met ali in lesson 2 as "no" and ala in lesson 10 as "there isn't". This lesson puts them side by side, adds a third, and settles which is which.

1. Three negators, one decision

graph TD Q{What are you negating?} -->|"an action or an identity"| A["ali
or the short e"] Q -->|"having something
or something existing"| B["ala"] A --> A1["Ali ya sinali — he did not buy"] B --> B1["Ala kung kwalta — I have no money"]

That is the whole decision. Doing takes ali; having takes ala. The third word, e, is simply a shorter ali.

2. ali — negating actions

Ali negates verbs and equations, and standing alone it means simply "no".

Ali ya sinali. — He did not buy.

Ali ku mipapatudtud. — I can't sleep.

Ali la bisang mangan. — They don't want to eat.

Note that the verb keeps its aspect. Sinali is still the completed form; ali does not flatten it into some neutral shape. "He did not buy" negates a completed event, and the grammar says so.

3. ala — negating having

Ala is the exact opposite of ati. Wherever you could have used ati, its negative is ala.

PositiveNegative
Ati kung kwalta. — I have money.Ala kung kwalta. — I have no money.
Ati ku keti. — I am here.Ala ku keti. — I am not here.

Ala katang nasi. — We two have no rice.

Ala tamung nasi. — We (including you) have no rice.

Ala keng nasi. — We (not including you) have no rice.

Obakit ala ka king bale yu? — Why are you not at your house?

Ala na mo kanung lugud. — They say there is no more love.

Those three "we have no rice" sentences are lesson 4's three-way we in action. They are not stylistic variants — they name three different groups of people.

Remember too that ala takes the special pronoun forms from lesson 10: ala ya / ala yu, ala la / ala lu.

4. e — the short one

E is a frequent everyday substitute for ali. It is not slang or careless; it is ordinary Kapampangan.

E ke seli. — I did not buy it.

E ku balu. — I don't know.

E ku pa menakit makanini. — I haven't seen one of these before.

Edaka buring mawala! — I don't want to lose you!

Look at E ke seli closely — it packs an enormous amount into three syllables. E (not) + ke (I … it, from lesson 5) + seli (bought, object focus, from lesson 11). Three words, three lessons, one small sentence.

💡 Why the negator comes first

Negators behave like verbs for word-order purposes: they take the front of the clause and the pronouns cluster right after them. That is why it is E ke seli and not Seli ke e.

Once you internalise "negator first, pronouns immediately after", a great deal of rapid Kapampangan speech becomes parseable.

6. Useful negatives

KapampanganEnglishBuilt from
ali panot yetali + pa (still)
ali nanot any moreali + na (already)
e ku baluI don't knowe + ku + balu (know)
alang problemano problemala + linker + problema

The particles pa ("still, else") and na ("now, already") are small but everywhere, and they flip meaning in the same way English "yet" and "any more" do.

E ku balu is probably the single most useful sentence in this course. Learn it before you need it — and you will need it.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Are ali and ala related? They look almost identical and differ by one vowel, which makes them easy to confuse in fast speech. Context resolves it: if a verb follows, it is ali; if a possession follows, it is ala.

When should I use e instead of ali? Freely. E is very common in speech. Ali is the safer choice when you want to be clearly understood or emphatic.

How do I say "don't!" as a command? Negative commands use e with the imperative. Lesson 17 covers commands in full.

Why This Lesson Matters

Beginners need negation earlier than they expect. "I don't know", "I don't have", "not yet" and "I don't understand" are the survival sentences of every language learner, and all four live in this lesson.
