English needs two completely different constructions for "there is a book" and "I have a book". Kapampangan uses one word for both, and it is one of the most useful words in the language.

1. ati — there is, and I have

ati = is present, is located  ·  atin = has

💡 One letter separates "is here" from "has"

Forman's grammar separates two jobs that look like one word:

 FormTakesExample
BE — present, locatedati the special pronouns yu / lu in the third person Ati ne i Ima. — Mother is here now.
HAVE — possessionatin the ordinary short pronouns, plus the -ng linker Atin yang barong Tagalog. — He has a barong Tagalog.

The negative, ala, covers both.

You will hear ati used loosely for both, and at A1 nobody will misunderstand you. But the -n is doing real work, and once you hear it you cannot unhear it.

Ati ku keti. — I am here. (BE)

Atin kung bale. — I have a house. (HAVE)

Ati la namang konsyensya. — They also have a conscience.

Obakit ati ka keni? — Why are you here?

Notice the range: existence ("there is"), possession ("I have") and location ("I am here") are all one construction. Kapampangan treats them as the same idea — something is present, whether in the world, in your hands, or in this room.

💡 There is no verb "to be"

Kapampangan has no equivalent of English "is". Masanting ya ing bale is literally "beautiful it the house" — no copula. Ati is not "to be" either; it is specifically about presence and possession.

This is normal across Philippine languages and takes a little while to stop feeling like something is missing. Nothing is missing.

2. ala — the negative

ala = there is not / there are none / to not have

Ala kung kwalta. — I have no money.

Ala katang nasi. — We two have no rice.

Ala na mo kanung lugud. — They say there is no more love.

Obakit ala ka king bale yu? — Why are you not at your house?

Ala is the exact opposite of ati, and it is not the same word as the general negator ali. The difference matters, and lesson 13 draws the line clearly. For now: ala negates having; ali negates doing.

3. The special pronoun forms

Here is a wrinkle. With ati and ala, the pronouns ya and la take alternative shapes. Both the regular and the special form are correct.

MeaningForms (both correct)
he/she/it is (present)ati ya  /  ati yu  /  atyu
they are (present)atila  /  atilu
he/she/it is notala ya  /  ala yu
they are notala la  /  ala lu

Ati yu king Pampanga. — He is in Pampanga.

Ala lu ring doktor keni. — The doctors are no longer here.

Atyu ku keni! — I am here!

Do not over-think this. Use ati ya and ala la if you want a single safe pattern; recognise atyu and ala lu when you hear them.

4. Ing bale — the house

KapampanganEnglish
balehouse
pasbuldoor
awangwindow

💡 bale is everywhere

Once you know bale, you start seeing it: kapitbale ("neighbour", lesson 7) is literally someone adjoining your house. Place names and surnames across Pampanga carry it too. It is a good example of how a small core vocabulary unlocks much more than its own meaning.

Nukarin la ring libro? — Where are the books?

Oita ya pala ing salamin mu! — So that's where your glasses are!

Luklukan is built from lukluk, "to sit" — a chair is a sitting-place. Dulang is the traditional low table people eat around while seated on the floor; it names a way of eating, not just a piece of furniture.

6. Two ways to say "my"

Lesson 4 introduced both. Here they are side by side, because this is the lesson where you will actually need the choice.

PatternExampleEffect
noun + doer pronouning bale kuneutral — "my house"
oblique pronoun + nouning kakung baleemphatic — "my house"
fuller oblique + nouning kanakung balesame, more formal

All three mean "my house". Use the first by default. Reach for the second when the ownership is the point — when you are contrasting it with someone else's.

7. Practice

Exercise 1: Have or have not

  • "I have a house." → "___ kung bale." (Answer: atin)
  • "I have no money." → "___ kung kwalta." (Answer: ala)
  • "I am here." → "___ ku keti." (Answer: ati)
  • "We two have no rice." → "___ katang nasi." (Answer: ala)

Exercise 2: The special forms

  • "He is in Pampanga." → "Ati ___ king Pampanga." (Answer: yu|ya)
  • "The doctors are no longer here." → "Ala ___ ring doktor keni." (Answer: lu|la)

Exercise 3: The house

  • house → ___ (Answer: bale)
  • door → ___ (Answer: pasbul)
  • window → ___ (Answer: awang)
  • kitchen → ___ (Answer: kusina)
  • chair → ___ (Answer: luklukan)

Exercise 4: Whose house?

  • "my house" (neutral) → "ing bale ___" (Answer: ku)
  • "MY house" (emphatic) → "ing ___ bale" (Answer: kakung|kanakung)
  • "your house" → "ing bale ___" (Answer: mu)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is ati a verb? Not really. It is an existential particle — a word class English does not have a clean name for. It does not conjugate for aspect the way lesson 11's verbs do.

ala or ali? Ala for not-having and not-existing; ali for not-doing. Ala kung kwalta ("I have no money") versus Ali ya sinali ("he did not buy"). Lesson 13 is entirely about this.

Why do ya and la change shape after ati? A historical fusion, much like the portmanteau pronouns in lesson 5. You do not need to derive it; you just need to recognise atyu and atilu as the same words you already know.
