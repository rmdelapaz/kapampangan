Ing, Ning, King — The Markers

Three small words that tell you who did what to whom

📑 In This Lesson

  5. di and kari — a person and their company

English works out who did what from position: "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" use identical words and mean opposite things. Kapampangan does not work that way. It labels each noun with a small word that states its job, and once the label is attached the noun can move around fairly freely.

Those labels are called markers, and there are only a handful. Learn them and Kapampangan sentences stop looking like word soup.

1. ing, ning, king

MarkerJobRough English
ingmarks the noun the sentence is about — the one in focus"the" (as subject)
ningmarks the doer when the doer is not the one in focus"by the", "of the"
kingmarks a location, a direction, or a recipient"to", "at", "in", "for"

Dintang ya ing lalaki.
The man arrived. — "the man" is what the sentence is about, so it takes ing.

Isulat ne ning lalaki ing poesia.
The boy will write the poem. — here the sentence is about the poem (so poesia takes ing), and the boy, though still the doer, drops to ning.

Ati ku king bale.
I am at the house. — location, so king.

💡 The key insight

Look again at the second example. The boy is doing the writing in both sentences — but which marker he gets depends on whether the sentence is about him or about the poem. Kapampangan lets you choose what to foreground, and the markers shift to follow your choice. That system is called focus, and it is the engine of the whole language. Lesson 12 opens it up properly.

2. When there is more than one

Each marker has a plural counterpart. Kapampangan freely alternates d- and r- forms — both are standard, and you will hear both from the same speaker.

JobSingularPlural
in focusingdeng / ding
out-of-focus doerningreng / ring
location / recipientkingkaring

Nukarin la ring libro? — Where are the books?

Mamangan la ring babi? — They eat pigs too?

Notice there is no plural -s anywhere. The marker carries the plurality, and the noun itself never changes shape.

💡 Variants you will hear

king very often turns up as keng (tatakut la keng kurus, "they're afraid of the cross"), and ning has the variants nang and neng. Forman treats each set as one case with several shapes rather than as separate words — so do not read keng or neng as something new.

3. People get their own markers

This is where Kapampangan diverges sharply from English and takes more care than Tagalog. A personal name never takes ing, ning or king. It takes a parallel series of its own.

JobCommon nounPersonal name
in focusing lalakii Juan
out-of-focus doerning lalakinang Juan
location / recipientking lalakikang Juan

Ikit neng Juan i Maria. — Juan saw Maria.

Nukarin ya i Henry? — Where is Henry?

Silatanan na kang José. — He wrote to José.

Ini ing asu nang Juan. — This is Juan's dog.

💡 A rule you must not skip

Look at Nukarin ya i Henry? — the pronoun ya is there even though "Henry" is named right beside it. Kapampangan requires the pronoun even when the noun is present. You cannot say Nukarin i Henry? any more than an English speaker can say "where Henry?"

This catches out every Tagalog speaker who comes to Kapampangan, because Tagalog does not do it. Lesson 4 drills it.

4. di and kari — a person and their company

Kapampangan has a neat device English lacks. Put di (or ri) before a name and it means "that person and the people with them".

di Juan — Juan and his household / Juan and company

Mamasa la ri Maria at Juan. — Maria and Juan are reading.

The matching location form is kari: kari Juan, "to Juan's place" or "to Juan and his people".

5. Why word order feels loose

Because the markers carry the grammar, Kapampangan can move nouns around for emphasis without creating confusion. The default order puts the verb first, but the nouns after it are not locked in place.

graph LR V[Verb first] --> M{Markers assign the roles} M --> A["ing — what the sentence is about"] M --> B["ning — the doer, backgrounded"] M --> C["king — where / to whom"] A --> R[Order can then flex for emphasis] B --> R C --> R

This is the payoff for learning the markers. They are not decoration you have to memorise; they are the thing doing the work that English does with word order and prepositions combined.

6. Practice

Exercise 1: Common-noun markers

  • "The man arrived." → "Dintang ya ___ lalaki." (Answer: ing)
  • "I am at the house." → "Ati ku ___ bale." (Answer: king)
  • "The boy will write the poem." → "Isulat ne ___ lalaki ing poesia." (Answer: ning)

Exercise 2: Singular to plural

  • ing libro → ___ libro (Answer: deng|ding)
  • ning babi → ___ babi (Answer: reng|ring)
  • king bale → ___ bale (Answer: karing)

Exercise 3: People, not things

  • "Where is Henry?" → "Nukarin ya ___ Henry?" (Answer: i)
  • "Juan's dog" → "ing asu ___ Juan" (Answer: nang)
  • "He wrote to José." → "Silatanan na ___ José." (Answer: kang)
  • "Juan and company" → "___ Juan" (Answer: di|ri)

Exercise 4: Spot the missing pronoun

Each sentence is missing the obligatory pronoun. Add it.

  • "Ernie arrived." → "Dintang ___ i Erning." (Answer: ya)
  • "Maria and Juan are reading." → "Mamasa ___ ri Maria at Juan." (Answer: la)

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Is ing just "the"? Not quite. It is closer to a spotlight than to an article — it says "this is the noun the sentence is built around." Kapampangan has no word that simply means "a" or "the" in the English sense.

deng or ding? reng or ring? Both, genuinely. The d- and r- forms alternate and neither is more correct. Pick one, stay consistent, and understand the other when you hear it.

What if I use the wrong marker? You will usually still be understood, because context does a lot of work. But swapping ing and ning can invert who did what to whom, so it is worth the care.

Why This Lesson Matters

Markers are the reason Kapampangan sentences that look scrambled to an English speaker are perfectly clear to a Kapampangan one. Every lesson from here on will use ing, ning and king constantly, and the focus system in lesson 12 is impossible without them.
